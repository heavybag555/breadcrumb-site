# 2u4u.studio — The Website

A companion document to the Personal Operating System and the essay *IE Blacktops, LA Terminals*. This one is about the website: what it is for, what each part does, and what it is meant to signal.

---

## Why the website exists

The studio could have introduced itself with a PDF, a slide deck, or a social handle. It introduces itself with a site because a site is the only deliverable that behaves like the work. You can click through it, read it at your own pace, return to it six months later, and see whether it has been cared for.

The site is the **deployment link** — the thing the essay argues for. Small brands and cultural projects don't hire a studio for its pitch deck; they hire it for its restraint, its coherence, its ability to hold a point of view across many small decisions. The only honest way to demonstrate that is to put the studio itself on the open web and let it be judged the way any studio's work is judged: by whether it feels considered.

## What the site is for

The website does three things at once:

1. **Presents the studio** — who runs it, what it makes, how to reach it.
2. **Publishes the point of view** — the philosophy, the principles, the commitments.
3. **Hosts the body of work** — client projects and photographic work, treated as one practice.

Everything on the site is in service of those three jobs. If a page doesn't do one of them, it doesn't belong.

---

## The homepage

The homepage opens with a single paragraph that assembles itself as you scroll:

> A web, interaction, and photography practice based in Los Angeles.
> Founded by Benjamin Uribe, the studio works across development, design, art direction, and photography — under one roof, one voice, one standard of care.

That sentence is the studio's working definition. It does not promise speed, scale, or breadth. It promises **one roof, one voice, one standard of care.** Those three phrases are the argument the rest of the site is trying to keep.

Below the paragraph, the work unfolds. Client websites and photography projects alternate down the page — not because it looks better that way, but because the studio is one practice. The site refuses the separation between "design work" and "personal work." They are the same work, made by the same person, held to the same standard.

## The work

Each project is presented with intention:

- The client's name and what was made.
- A short bio that treats the client as a real person, not a logo.
- The surface the project lives on — a domain, a shoot, a location.

There is no case-study theater here: no process-as-performance, no wall of deliverables, no forced narrative of "the challenge, the solution, the impact." The work stands, the client is named, and the domain is a live link. The visitor is trusted to draw their own conclusions.

This is a deliberate choice. The studio believes that **the work should be traceable back to a decision, not a preference** — and that if the work is good, it doesn't need to be sold; it needs to be shown.

## The info page

The info page is where the studio makes itself legible. It is organized into three rows — and the three rows map, on purpose, onto the three questions a serious client is actually asking.

**Information** — *Who is this, and can I reach them?*
A short biography, a list of services, a stack, a set of interests, and two lines of contact. Nothing more. No team page, no awards, no press. The studio is one person. The site admits it.

**Philosophy** — *What do they believe?*
Three paragraphs on the studio's working beliefs: that design is most useful when it is closest to the build; that quality is a structural decision, not a finishing pass; that the work should return something to the communities that don't always see themselves reflected in what gets built. Alongside them, the ten principles the studio operates by.

**Contribution to Humanity** — *What is this ultimately for?*
The studio exists at the intersection of two inheritances: a family of educators who gave knowledge away freely, and a field that has historically underrepresented the communities it claims to design for. The goal is to close that gap. Not as a mission statement. As a structural commitment.

## The principles

Ten sentences, numbered, sitting in their own column on the info page. They read like a short manifesto, but they are meant as a working contract — the rules the studio will be held to, project after project. They are the part of the site most likely to be quoted back to the studio later, which is the point.

1. Every project is a case study.
2. Coherence is a competitive advantage.
3. Ship something real before you call it a prototype.
4. The work should be traceable back to a decision, not a preference.
5. Quality is structural, not cosmetic.
6. Get close to the material — code is clay.
7. Urgency isn't always useful. Pause, think, then act.
8. Knowledge is most meaningful when it's given away.
9. Treat the end product as your own.
10. The community is the client too.

## The writing page

The writing page is where the studio publishes in long form — the essays, the process notes, the arguments that are too considered for a tweet and too specific for a portfolio caption. It is the slowest surface on the site, updated on a quarterly cadence rather than a daily one. The intention is that anyone who wants to understand how the studio thinks can read it end-to-end in under an hour.

## The resources page

The resources page credits the inputs — the people, courses, essays, and conversations the studio has been shaped by. It exists for two reasons. First, it is honest: no studio is self-made, and pretending otherwise is one of the small lies the design industry tells. Second, it is practical: a student in Hemet, California, who finds this site should be able to follow the same trail of references that brought the studio into being.

This page is the most literal expression of the principle **"knowledge is most meaningful when it's given away."**

## What the site is meant to signal

**To prospective clients →** that the studio has a point of view, will be direct about what it can and can't do, and is not afraid to publish the terms of engagement in advance.

**To peers in the field →** that independent practice is still possible; that a single designer-developer-photographer can hold a coherent voice without a team; that publishing early matters more than waiting until everything is polished.

**To the community the studio came from →** that someone who grew up in an Inland Empire computer lab can build the thing they wished they'd been able to find when they were ten. The site is here, publicly, permanently, for anyone who needs to see it to believe it.

---

## What the site is not

It is not a portfolio in the traditional sense. It is not a marketing funnel. It is not a content farm. It is not a proof of technical ability — although it is that as a byproduct.

It is a **studio introducing itself on its own terms**, on its own surface, in its own voice. That is the whole brief.

---

## The long view

The site will change. Projects will be added, principles will be refined, the essay page will fill up. The North Star will be revised every ninety days based on what the last ninety days taught the studio. But the center of the site — one roof, one voice, one standard of care — is fixed.

If, in five years, the studio has grown into the practice it intends to become, this site should still read as its earliest clear statement. And if, in five years, it has grown into something else entirely, this site will still be useful: it will be the record of what the studio believed when it was small, alone, and sure of itself.

That is why the website exists.
